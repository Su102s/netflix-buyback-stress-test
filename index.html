Is Netflix's buyback cash really spare?

Netflix says it only returns excess cash to shareholders. Over 2023–2025 that meant $21.4bn of share buybacks, 92% of its free cash flow. This page tests how much cash flow can fall before those buybacks break Netflix's own minimum-cash rule. You can move the assumptions yourself.

What makes Netflix fragile

  • The cushion is thin. Netflix held $9.1bn of cash and short-term investments at end-2025. Its own minimum is about $7.5bn, so only $1.5bn is spare.
  • Buybacks are six times the cushion. The $9.1bn buyback in 2025 was paid from that year's new cash flow, not from savings.
  • Costs are sticky. $11.5bn of content payments fall due within a year and are already signed. If revenue drops, these costs don't.
So: if cash flow falls, can Netflix keep buying back $9.1bn a year without dipping below its own emergency fund?

Recommendation

Hold management to its own rule

Netflix's own rule (keep about two months of revenue in cash) becomes a simple traffic light for each year's buybacks.

Continue

Free cash flow covers maturing debt plus planned buybacks (≥ 1.0×), and cash stays above the 2-month floor.

Slow down

Coverage falls below 1.0×. Fill the gap only with cash that sits above the floor.

Pause

Continuing would push cash below the floor. Stop buybacks rather than break the rule or borrow to fund them.

One more check sits on top: when the share price implies growth far above what is realistic (see below), keeping the cash or repaying debt may serve shareholders better.

Context · Do buybacks create value?

Higher EPS came from profit, not from fewer shares

Where EPS growth came from

Annual growth in diluted EPS, split into net-income growth and the extra boost from a smaller share count.

Net income growthBoost from fewer shares

Why the boost was so small

  • Netflix bought back 330 million shares over 2023–2025 (split-adjusted).
  • Shares outstanding fell by only 231 million, about 5%.
  • Roughly 3 in every 10 shares bought back were replaced by newly issued ones.
  • Buybacks shrink equity, so ROE rises almost automatically. Neither EPS nor ROE shows whether value was created. The price paid does.

What growth does the buyback price assume?

This runs the Gordon growth model backwards. Given the average price Netflix paid, its free cash flow per share and the return shareholders require, it solves for the perpetual growth that makes the price fair: g = (r·P − FCF) / (P + FCF).

Method

How the stress test works

The model (next 12 months, FY2025 base)

  • Cash flow before content = operating cash flow + content cash spend = $10.15bn + $17.71bn = $27.86bn, then reduced by the shock.
  • Content payments run from the 2025 level ($17.71bn) down to the signed minimum due within one year ($11.53bn), plus optional payments for unannounced titles.
  • Free cash flow = cash flow before content − content payments − capex ($0.69bn).
  • Bills = 2026 debt maturity ($1.0bn) + buybacks at the chosen pace (2025: $9.13bn).
  • Floor = two months of 2025 revenue ($45.18bn ÷ 6 = $7.53bn), against $9.06bn of cash and short-term investments.

Simplifications

  • The shock is applied directly to cash flow before content payments. Taxes, working capital and FX effects are not modelled.
  • Content payments are not deducted twice: they already sit inside operating cash flow.
  • No new borrowing is assumed. Netflix also has an undrawn $3bn revolving credit facility.
  • Cutting content to the signed minimum is an optimistic bound: less new content would hurt future subscribers and revenue.
  • The 10-K risk factors state that Netflix may be unable to reduce content commitments quickly if cash flow falls. That is the premise of this test.
